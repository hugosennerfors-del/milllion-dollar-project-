# What you actually sell

"A productized B2B service" is a category, not an offer. Here is the offer.

## The one sentence

> **You book qualified sales meetings for companies whose average deal is worth
> more than $40,000, on a $10,000/month retainer, with a guarantee.**

That is the whole business. Everything below is either the reason for a number
in that sentence or the mechanics of delivering it.

## Why meetings

Because it is the only product where **your one asset is the product itself.**
You have no technical skill, no domain expertise, and no audience. You can get
strangers to buy things. A company that needs pipeline is buying exactly that
ability, and you do not have to learn a new one to sell it.

It also has a property no other service has for you: **the sale is the demo.**
Every prospect who takes your call is on that call because your cold outreach
worked on them. You are not describing your product, they are standing inside
it. Nothing else you could sell gives you that on day one with zero credibility.

## Who you sell it to — the only filter that matters

A meeting is not worth a fixed amount. It is worth whatever it is worth to the
company receiving it:

```
meeting value = deal size × (meetings → opportunities) × (opportunities → won)
```

At typical rates (50% and 25%), a meeting is worth about **12.5% of one deal.**
Ten meetings a month is therefore worth about 1.25 deals a month in pipeline.
Invert that for a $10,000 retainer at 5× return — the point where price stops
being an argument — and you get a single, checkable rule:

> **Never put a company on your list unless its average deal is worth more than
> $40,000.**

Run `python3 run.py buyers` for the table. The result is not what most people
expect:

**B2B SaaS — the market everyone defaults to — is one of the harder ones at this
price.** The easy money is in unglamorous, high-ticket services that nobody
writes newsletters about. Series A startups are broke, over-solicited and
churn-prone. An MSP owner in Ohio has budget, a phone, and nobody courting them.

Those deal sizes are my estimates for sanity-checking a shortlist, not
researched figures. Confirm the one you pick before you build a list — it is
half a day of work and it decides your price.

## Picking your one niche

Take an "easy yes" row and cut it down until you can name 500 companies by hand.
Not "software agencies" — *Shopify-focused development agencies in the US and
Canada with 10–40 staff.*

If you have any connection to any industry at all — a friend, a former employer,
one company you understand — start there, even if its ROI is lower. Five
conversations go dramatically better when you know one true thing about the
business. **With no connection anywhere, take MSPs or development agencies**:
highly fragmented, owner-operated, buildable lists, and a pipeline problem they
already know they have.

Every market on that list is also targeted by other lead-gen agencies. Your edge
is not the niche, it is narrowness. Most of your competition is sending the same
mail-merge to "B2B companies."

## The problem you are actually worried about

Client one asks who else you have done this for, and you have nobody. This is
the real blocker, and there are three ways through it, in order of preference:

**1. Point at the call.** "You're speaking to me because a cold email worked on
you. That's the case study." It is true, it is unarguable, and it reframes the
missing track record as a live demonstration. Use this first, always.

**2. Sell the guarantee instead of the history.** *"Ten qualified meetings a
month. If we book fewer than eight, the next month is free."* You are not asking
them to believe you, you are removing the reason to care. Define "qualified"
precisely and in writing — right title, right company size, showed up, agreed to
a next step — or you will argue about it in month two.

**3. Price on performance for the first two clients only.** *"$1,000 per
qualified meeting, no retainer."* Same money at ten meetings, none of the risk
for them. Use it to buy your first two logos, then stop — performance pricing
does not build the recurring revenue an acquirer pays for, and two-thirds of
your odds live in the sale.

Never discount the retainer to win client one. A cheap client is a permanent
anchor on your pricing and the first thing a buyer of your business will notice.

## Who does the work

Not you, past the first two clients:

| Role | What they do | Rough cost |
|---|---|---|
| List builder / researcher (VA) | Sourcing, enrichment, verification | $6–12/hr |
| Inbox manager / SDR | Sequences, replies, booking | $10–20/hr |
| Tooling | Domains, mailboxes, data, sequencer | $300–500/mo per client |

Roughly 60–80 contractor hours a month per client, so **$2,000–3,500 all-in
against a $10,000 retainer.** The financial model assumes 42–45% delivery cost,
so the model is the conservative version of this — which is how you want the
error to point.

You keep two things and delegate everything else: **the sales calls, and the
offer.** Hand over a call and you have given away the business.

## What the first email looks like

Short, specific, one ask, a number in it:

> **Subject:** 10 meetings for Northbound?
>
> Sam — you're on my list because Northbound's Clutch profile says you take on
> 8–10 Shopify builds a year, and you've had two developer roles open since
> August.
>
> Most agencies your size run on referrals, which works right up until a quarter
> goes quiet and the bench is expensive.
>
> We book qualified meetings with ecommerce directors who have budget approved
> and a project starting inside 90 days. Ten a month. If we book fewer than
> eight, you don't pay for the next one.
>
> Worth fifteen minutes on Thursday?

Four things doing the work: a specific true observation, their problem in their
language, one promise with a number, and a guarantee that removes the risk of
you having no history. Not a single adjective about yourself.

## What you do not sell

**Anything paid per transaction.** Recruiting fits your skill well and pays
enormously — $20–40k a placement — but it is lumpy, non-recurring revenue, and
buyers pay a fraction of the multiple for it. Two-thirds of your odds come from
selling the business, and businesses sell on recurring revenue. That single
constraint rules out the entire category, however good the monthly income looks.

**Anything under $6,000 a month.** Covered in `00-the-arithmetic.md`: below that,
the business you would need is larger than the one you can staff at 20 hours a
week.

**Anything you have to become an expert in first.** You have 24 months. Spending
six of them learning an industry is spending a quarter of your runway on
homework.
